Dear Prof. Gagliardi,

I am submitting the manuscript "When is committee force uncertainty worth
computing in MD? Decision quality, cheap gates, and the baselines that must be
beaten" for consideration as an Article in the Journal of Chemical Theory and
Computation.

Ensembles of machine-learned interatomic potentials are the standard way to decide
which structures to label next in an active-learning cycle, and a substantial
literature now builds cheap approximations to the ensemble's force disagreement.
That literature validates each approximation by checking that it reproduces the
exact ensemble. The manuscript argues that this is the wrong target, because the
ensemble is itself only a proxy for the model's error against the reference
calculation, and it separates the two questions that the validation conflates:
whether exact committee disagreement predicts reference-force error at all, and
what a cheap gate costs relative to whichever oracle one has adopted. The second
answer is independent of the first, so it survives the first being disappointing.

Three findings seem worth the journal's attention.

First, the acquisition rule used in prior work sums per-coordinate variance over
all coordinates and is therefore extensive. It wins on every comparison we ran at
fixed system size and then reverses once system size varies, acquiring structures
whose median atom count is 2.5 times larger rather than structures that are harder.
The recommendation was never wrong where it had been measured; it had only been
measured where the pathology cannot appear. We give a size normalisation, fitted on
a design split and reported on the disjoint remainder, that restores parity at
variable size and changes nothing at fixed size.

Second, a deterministic leading-subspace estimator outperforms a randomised
low-rank sketch at matched cost on five of six systems, and an exact finite-sample
law predicts the randomised estimator's failure to a mean absolute error of 0.015
with no free parameters. This is a negative result for randomisation in a setting
where randomisation is the default choice.

Third, and least flattering to the method the work began by trying to accelerate:
on 136,923 MPtraj crystals evaluated with a frozen foundation-model trunk, the
maximum mean-force norm - which costs nothing, being already computed in the
forward pass - reaches AUROC 0.943 against the committee's 0.798, and adding the
committee on top of it changes that by 0.0002, which is not significant. The
advantage survives stratification by atom count and by force-magnitude quintile,
so neither confound explains it. On fixed-composition systems the committee does
add significantly. Committee uncertainty is therefore not redundant in general; it
is redundant where a free signal is already near-saturated, and we argue that
future claims in this area should be scored against that free signal rather than
against chance.

The work fits the journal's stated interest in artificial intelligence and data
science for chemical theory and computation, and in molecular dynamics
methodology. It is not an application of an established method to a single system
class: the systems span 9- to 24-atom organic molecules, bulk liquid water with an
MD-versus-PIMD distribution shift, and periodic crystals spanning 89 elements and
1 to 444 atoms.

On reproducibility, which the manuscript treats as a first-class concern: every
numeric value in the text is generated from a committed experimental record rather
than transcribed, and a procedural audit verifies that each still derives, that
every pre-registered protocol is hash-bound to a registration record written before
that experiment produced any artifact, and that every data split recomputes from
its recorded seed. All statistical results regenerate on CPU in roughly four
minutes with no model execution and no downloads. The manuscript also contains a
section disclosing the use of large language models in this work, along with the
verification machinery that disclosure makes necessary.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. A shorter version of the
earlier, workshop-scale portion of this work has been submitted to machine-learning
workshops, all of which are non-archival and none of which publishes proceedings;
the present manuscript supersedes that material and contains substantial results
not present in it, including the foundation-model and crystal experiments, the
extensivity analysis, the deterministic estimator, and the finite-sample law. I am
happy to provide further detail on that relationship if it would be useful.

I have no competing financial interests to declare. Thank you for considering the
